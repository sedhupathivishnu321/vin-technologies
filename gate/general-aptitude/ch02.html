Chapter 2

Comprehension, Sentence Completion and Verbal Reasoning

In this chapter

  2. 2.2 Sentence completion
  3. 2.3 Narrative sequencing
  4. 2.4 Verbal reasoning
  5. 2.5 GATE problems
GATE Focus

Short-passage comprehension (main idea, inference, vocabulary in context), sentence completion by contextual clues, rearranging jumbled sentences into a logical narrative, and verbal reasoning (inferences from statements, strengthen/weaken).

2.1 Reading comprehension

Method for a short passage

1. Read the passage once for the main idea (the first and last sentences usually carry it). 2. Read each question, then return to the specific lines. 3. Answer from the passage only, never from outside knowledge. 4. Eliminate extreme words (always, never, only, all) unless the passage uses them.
Question types: main idea / title; stated detail; inference (“can be concluded”); tone and attitude; vocabulary in context; “NOT / EXCEPT”.

Worked passage

“Cities are usually warmer than the countryside around them. Dark roads and roofs absorb sunlight and release it as heat after sunset, while concrete and asphalt replace the plants that once cooled the air through evaporation. Tall buildings also block the wind, so warm air lingers between them. Engineers have begun to respond: light-coloured roofing, street trees and permeable pavements can lower surface temperatures by several degrees, but such measures work best when adopted across whole neighbourhoods rather than in isolated buildings.”
Main idea: cities trap heat for identifiable reasons, and coordinated design can reduce it. NOT mentioned as a cause: vehicle exhaust. “Lingers” means: stays. Inference: one cool roof on its own will help less than a whole neighbourhood of them.

2.2 Sentence completion

Clue types

Contrast (although, but, however, yet): the blank has the opposite sense of the other part. Cause–effect (because, so, therefore): the blank continues the same line of reasoning. Definition / restatement (that is, a kind of): the blank repeats the meaning. Parallelism: the blank matches the tone of its list.

Test each option by reading the whole sentence; the word must fit the logic, the tone and the grammar. Example: “Although the proposal seemed at first, closer examination revealed serious flaws”: although signals contrast with “flaws”, so the blank is promising.

2.3 Narrative sequencing

Ordering jumbled sentences

Find the opening sentence: it introduces the topic with no pronoun or connector (it, this, however, as a result). Follow chronology or cause \(\to\) effect. A pronoun must come after the noun it replaces. Connectors fix local order: as a result, therefore, but, then, finally. Check the final order by reading it aloud.

Solved Example

P. As a result, the village was abandoned. Q. A long drought dried the river. R. Farmers could no longer irrigate their fields. S. The village had once thrived on the river’s water.
S introduces the topic; Q gives the cause; R the immediate effect; P the outcome (“as a result”). Order: S Q R P.

2.4 Verbal reasoning

Reasoning from statements

Syllogism-style inferences: “All A are B” + “Some B are C” gives no certain relation between A and C. “No A is B” \(\Rightarrow\) “No B is A”. “All A are B” \(\Rightarrow\) “Some B are A” (the converse of “all” is only “some”).
Strengthen / weaken: a claim is weakened by showing the stated cause is absent, or an alternative cause exists; strengthened by evidence that the link is present. Assumptions are unstated premises the argument needs. Conclusions must follow from the statements alone.

Conceptual Trap

(i) Choosing an answer that is true in the real world but not in the passage. (ii) Extreme options (“always”, “only”) in inference questions. (iii) A conclusion that goes beyond the given statements. (iv) Ordering by the connector “however” as the first sentence; a connector never opens a passage. (v) Treating “All A are B” as “All B are A”.

GATE Shortcut

For comprehension, locate the answer line before reading the options. For sentence completion, decide whether the clue is contrast or continuation first. For sequencing, build the first two sentences and match the options. For reasoning, draw a Venn diagram (see Chapter 9) or test each option with a counter-example.

2.5 GATE problems

GATE 1-Mark Problems

Q1. “Although the proposal seemed at first, closer examination revealed serious flaws.” (A) flawed (B) promising (C) absurd (D) incomplete.

Q2. “The scientist’s findings were so that other laboratories could reproduce them exactly.” (A) ambiguous (B) controversial (C) reliable (D) trivial.

Q3. Which of the following sentences must begin a paragraph? (A) However, the results were poor. (B) This delayed the project. (C) Solar power is growing quickly. (D) As a result, costs fell.

Q4. From “All snakes are reptiles” it follows that (A) all reptiles are snakes (B) some reptiles are snakes (C) no reptile is a snake (D) all reptiles are cold-blooded.

Answers: Q1 (B). Q2 (C). Q3 (C): the others begin with a connector or pronoun. Q4 (B).

GATE 2-Mark Problems

Q5. Using the passage on city heat above, the passage suggests that (A) planting a single tree solves urban heating (B) neighbourhood-wide measures are more effective than isolated ones (C) wind raises city temperatures (D) countryside is warmer than cities.

Q6. Arrange P. “Rust therefore weakens the steel.” Q. “Iron in steel reacts with oxygen and water.” R. “Engineers protect bridges with paint and galvanising.” S. “This produces iron oxide, commonly called rust.” (A) QSPR (B) QPSR (C) SQPR (D) RQSP.

Q7. A company claims its battery lasts longer because of a new electrode. Which finding most weakens the claim? (A) Customers like the design. (B) Old and new electrodes perform equally in identical test cells. (C) The new battery costs more. (D) The company has expanded overseas.

Answers: Q5 (B). Q6 (A): Q (reaction), S (product), P (effect), R (remedy). Q7 (B).

Numerical Answer Type (NAT)

Q8. Of ENLIST, TINSEL, INLETS and STONE, the number that are anagrams of LISTEN is .

Q9. The number of nouns in “The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog” is .

Q10. The number of adjectives in the same sentence is .

Answers: Q8: 3. Q9: 2 (fox, dog). Q10: 3 (quick, brown, lazy).

Multiple Select (MSQ)

Q11. “No reptile is warm-blooded; all snakes are reptiles.” Which are valid? (A) No snake is warm-blooded. (B) All reptiles are snakes. (C) Some reptiles are not warm-blooded. (D) No warm-blooded animal is a snake.

Answers: Q11: (A), (C), (D).

Chapter Test

T1. What does “however” tell you about a sentence’s position?   T2. “All A are B” and “Some B are C”: is “Some A are C” certain?   T3. Which clue type is “although”?

Answers: T1: it follows an earlier contrasting sentence, never first. T2: no. T3: contrast.

One-Page Revision
  • Passage: main idea first; answer only from the text; beware extreme words.
  • Sentence completion: classify the clue (contrast / cause / definition) then test the options.
  • Sequencing: opening sentence has no connector; pronoun after its noun; chronology or cause \(\to\) effect.
  • Reasoning: converse of “all” is “some”; weaken by removing the link or giving a rival cause.
