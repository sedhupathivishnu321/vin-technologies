How to use

Each trap is a way of losing marks while knowing the material. For each: what goes wrong, why, a numerical illustration, and the habit that avoids it. Read this chapter the night before the exam.

35.1 Sign conventions

Trap. \(p=vi\) is absorbed power only when the current enters the \(+\) terminal (passive sign convention). Example. \(v=6\) V, current \(2\) A leaving the \(+\) terminal: the element delivers \(12\) W. Habit. Mark polarity and current arrow on the element before writing \(vi\); a resistor can never deliver power.

35.2 Dependent sources

Trap. Zeroing a dependent source when applying superposition or finding \(R_{Th}\). Why. Only independent sources are “turned off”; a dependent source is a circuit element controlled by another variable. Example. \(V_{oc}=4\) V, \(I_{sc}=5\) A gives \(R_{Th}=0.8\ \Omega\), but shorting the dependent source would give a different (wrong) number. Habit. Use \(V_{oc}/I_{sc}\) or the test-source method whenever a dependent source is present. Bonus. Count unknowns: one extra equation for the controlling variable.

35.3 RMS versus average

Trap. Using \(V_m/\sqrt2\) for every waveform, or the average where RMS is asked. Example. Half-wave rectified \(10\) V: average \(3.18\), RMS \(5\), not \(7.07\). Full-wave: average \(6.37\), RMS \(7.07\). Habit. Power questions need RMS; DC-meter questions need average; \(V_{rms}^2=V_0^2+\sum V_k^2/2\). And decide peak vs RMS phasors before writing \(P_{max}=V^2/8R\) (peak) or \(V^2/4R\) (rms).

35.4 Power versus apparent power

Trap. Adding \(|S|\) of loads or using \(S\) where \(P\) is asked. Example. \(12.5\) kVA and \(8.33\) kVA loads in parallel give \(15.02\) kVA, not \(20.8\) kVA; add \(P\) and \(Q\) separately. Habit. Work with \(P+jQ\); pf \(=P/S\); lagging or leading is decided by the sign of \(Q\).

35.5 Initial versus final conditions

Trap. Assuming inductor voltage or capacitor current is continuous; using the wrong circuit for \(\tau\). Example. \(L\) carrying \(3\) A switched across \(6\ \Omega\): \(v_L(0^-)=0\) but \(v_L(0^+)=-18\) V. Habit. \(v_C\) and \(i_L\) are continuous; every other quantity may jump. Time constant from the circuit after the switch moves; \(x(\infty)\) from C open, L short.

35.6 Causality versus stability

Trap. Equating them. Example. \(e^{t}u(t)\) is causal but unstable; \(e^{-|t|}\) is non-causal but stable; an integrator is causal and BIBO unstable. Habit. Causal \(\Leftrightarrow h=0\) for negative time (ROC to the right of all poles); stable \(\Leftrightarrow\int|h|<\infty\) (ROC contains \(j\omega\) or the unit circle). Both together for causal systems: poles in the open LHP (or inside the unit circle).

35.7 Energy versus power signals

Trap. Calling \(u(t)\) or a periodic signal an energy signal; calling \(r(t)\) a power signal. Example. \(u(t)\): \(P=\tfrac12\), \(E=\infty\). \(r(t)\) and \(e^{t}u(t)\): neither. \(\delta[n]\): energy (\(E=1\)). Habit. Compute \(E\); if infinite, compute \(P\); if that is also infinite, neither.

35.8 Nyquist rate versus Nyquist frequency

Trap. Confusing \(2f_m\) (rate: samples/s needed) with \(f_s/2\) (frequency: the highest representable at a given \(f_s\)). Example. Band-limited to \(4\) kHz: Nyquist rate \(8\) kHz; at \(f_s=8\) kHz the Nyquist frequency is \(4\) kHz. Products add bandwidths; convolutions take the minimum. Habit. Write \(f_m\) first (in Hz!), then \(2f_m\); alias with \(|f-kf_s|\).

35.9 DTFT versus DFT

Trap. Treating the DFT as the DTFT. The DTFT is continuous in \(\Omega\) and \(2\pi\)-periodic for any sequence; the DFT is \(N\) samples of the DTFT of a length-\(N\) sequence, with circular (not linear) convolution. Example. \(\{1,2,3,4\}\circledast\{1,1,0,0\}=\{5,3,5,7\}\) but the linear convolution is \(\{1,3,5,7,4\}\). Habit. For linear convolution via DFT use \(N\ge L_x+L_h-1\).

35.10 ROC versus pole locations

Trap. Reading the sequence from \(X(z)\) (or \(X(s)\)) without the ROC; assuming the ROC is always exterior. Example. \(\frac1{1-0.5z^{-1}}\) is \((0.5)^nu[n]\) for \(|z|>0.5\) but \(-(0.5)^nu[-n-1]\) for \(|z|<0.5\). A stable, two-sided system can have poles inside and outside the unit circle. Habit. The ROC never contains a pole; right-sided \(\to\) outside the outermost pole.

35.11 FIR versus IIR stability

Trap. Worrying about FIR stability (it is unconditional), or assuming an IIR system is stable because its coefficients are small. Example. \(y[n]=1.2y[n-1]+x[n]\) has \(h=1.2^nu[n]\): unstable. A causal IIR filter is stable iff all poles \(|p|<1\). Exactly linear phase is possible only for FIR (causal, real, stable).

35.12 Other high-frequency slips

  • \(\delta(at)=\frac1{|a|}\delta(t)\) (absolute value).
  • \(\sinc(x)=\sin\pi x/\pi x\): the GATE normalisation; \(\rect(t/\tau)\leftrightarrow\tau\sinc(\omega\tau/2\pi)\).
  • DT periodicity requires \(\Omega_0/2\pi\) rational.
  • Superposition applies to \(v,i\), not power.
  • The final-value theorem needs poles of \(sX\) in the open LHP (or \(z\): inside the unit circle).
  • ABCD uses \(-I_2\); cascade order matters.
  • Group delay \(=-d\phi/d\omega\), phase delay \(=-\phi/\omega\).
  • Half-wave symmetry: \(x(t+T/2)=-x(t)\) \(\Rightarrow\) odd harmonics only.
